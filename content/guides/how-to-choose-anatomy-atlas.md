## Illustrated vs photographic atlases

The first decision is the type of image, not the brand.

- **Illustrated atlases** use drawings. Colors separate arteries, veins and nerves, and clutter is removed, so relationships are easy to see.
- **Photographic atlases** show dissected cadavers. Structures look the way they will in the dissection lab and on practical exams, which also makes them harder to read at first.

## The three standard options

| Atlas | Image type | Strength | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Netter, *Atlas of Human Anatomy* | Hand-painted illustrations | Clear, widely used, matches many courses | Little explanatory text |
| Rohen, *Anatomy: A Photographic Atlas* | Cadaver photographs | Closest to what you see in the lab | Steeper learning curve |
| Thieme (Gilroy), *Atlas of Anatomy* | Modern illustrations | Summary tables and clinical notes alongside images | Larger and heavier |

{{< amazon q="Thieme Atlas of Anatomy Gilroy" text="The Thieme Atlas of Anatomy" >}} adds tables of muscle origins, insertions and innervation next to the images, so you look up less elsewhere.

## An atlas is not a textbook

An atlas shows you where things are. It does not explain function or clinical relevance. For that you need a text such as {{< amazon q="Moore Clinically Oriented Anatomy" text="Moore's Clinically Oriented Anatomy" >}} or {{< amazon q="Gray's Anatomy for Students" text="Gray's Anatomy for Students" >}}. Check whether your course expects both before you spend money on either.

## How to decide

1. Ask how your practical exam is run: tagged cadavers, models or images.
2. If cadavers, plan to use a photographic atlas at least for revision.
3. Look at the same region (the brachial plexus is a good test) in each atlas at the library and pick the one you find easiest to read.
