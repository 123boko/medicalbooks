## Why the reading list is misleading

A course reading list is written to be complete. It names the standard reference for every subject so that no student is left without a source. It is not a shopping list, and lecturers generally do not expect you to own every title on it.

In practice, most teaching now comes through lecture slides, recorded sessions and course notes. Textbooks are what you open when those leave a gap.

## What students actually use

How much you use textbooks depends on the subject.

| Subject | How textbooks are typically used |
|---|---|
| Anatomy | An atlas is used constantly, in the lab and for revision |
| Physiology | A readable text helps when a concept will not click |
| Pathology | Often learned from review resources, with a textbook for lookup |
| Pharmacology | A text or review book is useful for drug classes and mechanisms |
| Clinical years | Short handbooks and online references are used more than large texts |

## Books worth owning

- **An anatomy atlas.** You will use it almost daily during the anatomy block and it is awkward to share. See [how to choose an anatomy atlas]({{< relref "how-to-choose-anatomy-atlas.md" >}}).
- **One book for your weakest subject.** If physiology is hard for you, a clear physiology text earns its price. If it comes easily, skip it.
- **Your main exam review book**, bought in the current edition when you start preparing.

## Books to borrow or read online

- **Large references**, such as the full pathology or internal medicine texts. You will look things up in them; you will not read them through.
- **Anything you need for one module only.**

Before buying anything, check what your school library provides. Many medical schools license the standard textbooks as e-books, and you can read them free with your student login.

## Four checks before you buy

1. **Wait two weeks.** By then you will know which books lecturers actually refer to.
2. **Ask students in the year above.** They know which titles were used and which stayed on the shelf.
3. **Check the library and its e-book collection.**
4. **Consider an older edition** for subjects that change slowly. Our guide to [older editions]({{< relref "older-edition-medical-textbooks.md" >}}) lists which subjects are safe.

## When buying does make sense

Some people learn better from a physical book they can mark up, and some schools have limited library access. If you know you will read a book repeatedly, owning it is reasonable. The point is to buy because you will use it, not because it is on a list.

## A sensible first-year purchase list

For a typical first year, this is often enough to start:

- one anatomy atlas;
- one anatomy textbook if your course assigns readings ([our comparison]({{< relref "best-anatomy-textbook-medical-students.md" >}}));
- one physiology text if you want one ([Guyton vs Costanzo]({{< relref "guyton-vs-costanzo-physiology.md" >}})).

Add to it only when a course gives you a reason.
