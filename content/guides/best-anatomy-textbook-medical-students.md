## What a medical student needs from an anatomy textbook

An anatomy textbook has a different job from an atlas. The atlas shows you where a structure is. The textbook tells you what it does, what runs next to it, and what goes wrong when it is injured. Exam questions are written from that second kind of knowledge: a patient cannot extend the wrist, which nerve is damaged and where?

So the test for any anatomy text is simple. Open the chapter on the upper limb and see whether it explains nerve injuries in a way you could repeat to a classmate.

## The three standard choices

| Textbook | Approach | Strength | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moore, *Clinically Oriented Anatomy* | Regional, heavy clinical correlation | Clinical boxes on almost every page | Long; easy to get lost in detail |
| Drake, *Gray's Anatomy for Students* | Regional, concept first | Clear modern diagrams, readable | Less clinical depth than Moore |
| Snell, *Clinical Anatomy by Regions* | Regional, concise | Direct, exam-friendly explanations | Plainer illustrations |

### Moore's Clinically Oriented Anatomy

{{< book q="Moore Clinically Oriented Anatomy" title="Clinically Oriented Anatomy" short="Clinically Oriented Anatomy" by="Moore, Dalley and Agur" for="students who want clinical context on every topic" >}}

Moore is the book most North American courses are built around. Its trademark is the clinical box: short passages set apart from the main text that connect a structure to an injury, a procedure or an examination finding. Those boxes are where a lot of exam questions come from.

The cost is length. It is a book to read in assigned sections, not from the first page to the last.

### Gray's Anatomy for Students

{{< book q="Gray's Anatomy for Students" title="Gray's Anatomy for Students" short="Gray's for Students" by="Drake, Vogl and Mitchell" for="students who learn from diagrams and want less dense text" >}}

Despite the name, this is not a shortened version of the big reference Gray's. It was written from scratch for students. Each region opens with a conceptual overview before the detail, and the illustrations are drawn in one consistent style, which makes them easy to compare across chapters. We cover the difference in [Gray's Anatomy vs Gray's Anatomy for Students]({{< relref "grays-anatomy-vs-grays-anatomy-for-students.md" >}}).

### Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions

{{< book q="Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions" title="Clinical Anatomy by Regions" short="Clinical Anatomy by Regions" by="Richard S. Snell" for="short courses and students who want a direct, compact text" >}}

Snell gets to the point quickly and keeps clinical notes close to the anatomy they relate to. Students who find Moore overwhelming often do better with it.

## If your anatomy course is short

Many schools now teach anatomy in a compressed block. In that case the full Moore is more than you can use. {{< amazon q="Moore Essential Clinical Anatomy" text="Essential Clinical Anatomy" >}}, the condensed version by the same authors, keeps the clinical boxes and drops much of the detail.

## Do you need a textbook and an atlas?

Usually yes, but not two textbooks. One text plus one atlas covers nearly every course. If you have not chosen an atlas yet, see [how to choose an anatomy atlas]({{< relref "how-to-choose-anatomy-atlas.md" >}}).

## How to decide

1. Check which book your course assigns readings from. That settles most cases.
2. If nothing is assigned, read the brachial plexus section in Moore and in Gray's for Students at the library.
3. Buy the one you understood on the first read. Anatomy does not change between editions, so [an older edition is usually fine]({{< relref "older-edition-medical-textbooks.md" >}}).
